I should get one thing out of the way before talking about the architecture, the rule engine, the testing, or anything else.

Yojana Sathi was vibe-coded.

I did not sit down and manually type thousands of lines of Python from scratch. Claude Code and ChatGPT helped write essentially the entire software project: application code, tests, deployment scripts, documentation, parts of the website, refactors, and fixes.

Sometimes I described what I wanted and let Claude Code implement most of it.

Sometimes I gave it a bug and asked it to trace the cause.

Sometimes ChatGPT acted as an outside reviewer and found things that both Claude and I had missed.

So I do not want to publish a project story that quietly removes AI from the picture and makes it sound as though I hand-crafted every function myself. That would be dishonest.

But saying “AI built it” would also leave out most of what actually happened.

AI could generate the code. It could suggest an architecture. It could write tests. It could read a stack trace faster than I could.

It could not decide which government source I was willing to trust.

It could not test the Telegram bot on my phone and notice that tapping an old button changed the answer to a completely different question.

It could not decide whether an ambiguous pension rule was safe enough to serve to a worker.

It could not log into my AWS server, look at the thing as a real deployed system, understand what I was comfortable putting in front of people, or take responsibility if the answer was wrong.

And that difference ended up becoming the most interesting part of the entire project.

This is the story of how Yojana Sathi went from an AI-generated hackathon project into something I could actually reason about, test, deploy, break, fix, and explain.

It is also a record of the mistakes.

There were quite a few.

Where the idea came from

Yojana Sathi was not actually the first project I started for the hackathon.

Before it, I built another project called Saans, an air-exposure assistant.

I had approached the hackathon quite analytically. I looked at the available problem areas, their scale, how much evidence I could find, and how a project might score. Clean air looked strong, so I built around it.

Technically, Saans went pretty far.

But I eventually noticed a problem that had nothing to do with its code.

I was spending my time improving something that did not yet have meaningful user evidence.

That distinction started bothering me.

A project can have a nice architecture, a large test suite, a polished README, and thousands of lines of working code while still having no proof that anybody actually needs it.

So I parked Saans.

That felt wasteful at first. I had already spent time on it.

Later I realised it gave me the most useful lesson I could have carried into the next project:

shipping and learning from reality matter more than making the repository look finished.

When I began looking for another problem, I started thinking about government welfare schemes.

India has an enormous unorganised workforce. A Ministry of Labour and Employment parliamentary reply, citing the Economic Survey 2021–22, put the number at about 43.99 crore workers in 2019–20.

The infrastructure around welfare schemes already exists. e-Shram alone had more than 31.48 crore registered workers by January 2026, with multiple central schemes connected to it.

So the problem was clearly not that India had no welfare programmes.

The more interesting problem was what happens between a scheme existing and somebody actually using it.

Government rules are scattered across portals, PDFs, FAQs, departments, state websites, and sometimes documents that are difficult to find or interpret.

A worker may know a scheme exists without knowing whether it applies to them.

They may know they probably qualify without knowing which documents are needed.

Or they may travel to a Common Service Centre and discover that one missing condition makes them ineligible.

The cost of that trip is not abstract.

The Government's 2025 labour-force data reported average daily earnings for casual labour at roughly ₹455 for men and ₹315 for women.

That gave me a much better way to think about the product.

A wrong answer from my bot was not merely a bad chatbot response.

It could cost somebody a working day.

That became the idea behind Yojana Sathi:

Know before you go.

A worker answers a small number of questions in Hindi or English. The system checks those answers against welfare-scheme rules, explains what it can determine, tells the worker what documents to carry, and points them toward the next step.

I was not trying to rebuild the government's existing portals.

I wanted to build a conversational last-mile layer around the information that already exists.

Telegram was the easiest place to start.

WhatsApp came later because that is much closer to where the intended users actually are.

The first important decision had nothing to do with AI

It would have been very tempting to build Yojana Sathi as an AI chatbot.

A worker types:

I am 32, work in construction, earn ₹12,000 and don't have EPFO. Which schemes can I get?

An LLM reads the government information and responds.

That would have taken very little time to prototype.

It also scared me.

The problem was not whether a modern model could get most answers right.

It probably could.

The problem was what happens on the answer it gets wrong.

If an LLM invents an income threshold, forgets an exclusion, confuses two pension schemes, or confidently fills a missing detail, the response can still sound completely reasonable.

The person discovering the mistake might not be me.

It might be somebody standing at a government counter after losing a day of work.

So I made a rule:

The AI is not allowed to decide eligibility.

The eligibility engine would be ordinary deterministic Python.

Same inputs, same output.

No randomness.

No network request.

No model call.

The rules themselves would live in plain TOML files.

The LLM could help with language or classify some free text, but it could not produce the final verdict.

That created a useful separation:

Worker
   ↓
Telegram / WhatsApp
   ↓
Conversation flow
   ↓
Profile facts
   ↓
Deterministic rule engine
   ↓
ELIGIBLE / INELIGIBLE / UNKNOWN
   ↓
Human-readable explanation

The model could participate around the edges.

It could never cross into the part where the answer became yes or no.

There is even a test that checks the rule package does not import an LLM or networking code.

That may sound excessive for a portfolio project.

After everything that happened later, it is one of the decisions I am happiest I made.

Three answers instead of two

The next decision came from the same concern.

Most eligibility systems naturally want to return a Boolean:

true
false

Eligible or not eligible.

But real government rules are messy.

What happens if the worker does not know whether their NPS account receives a government contribution?

What happens if I have not yet confirmed one exclusion against a reliable source?

What if two official sources disagree?

Turning those cases into false is dangerous.

Turning them into true is worse.

So Yojana Sathi uses three results:

ELIGIBLE
INELIGIBLE
UNKNOWN

UNKNOWN is not treated as an error.

It is a legitimate result.

Sometimes the safest answer a system can give is:

I cannot confirm this from what we know. Ask this exact question at the office.

I gradually came to see that as one of the strongest ideas in the project.

Software loves certainty.

Reality does not always provide it.

Instead of hiding uncertainty, I wanted the system to carry it all the way to the user.

Another strange choice: "TODO" is data

There was another tiny design decision that became surprisingly useful.

Suppose I am researching a scheme and still do not know its annual benefit value.

The obvious placeholder might be:

annual_value_inr = 0

But zero is valid data.

There are schemes such as registrations or gateways where the correct cash value genuinely can be zero.

If I use zero to mean “I haven't researched this yet,” the program cannot tell those two meanings apart.

So incomplete values are deliberately ugly:

annual_value_inr = "TODO"

A string inside a field that normally contains a number.

That is intentional.

The loader knows "TODO" means the information has not been researched.

The engine knows it must not quietly convert that into a verdict.

A missing fact becomes visible instead of looking finished.

This principle kept appearing throughout the project:

If an unsafe unfinished state looks like valid data, eventually somebody will treat it as valid data.

The honest part about using Claude Code and ChatGPT

This project was built during the period when AI coding tools had become good enough that I could work much faster than my own coding speed.

I leaned into that.

Claude Code became the main implementation tool.

I would give it the repository, explain the feature or bug, tell it what constraints could not change, ask it to inspect the relevant files, and then let it implement the change.

For anything larger than a tiny edit, I found it worked much better when I asked it to plan first.

Read the files.

Tell me what will change.

Tell me which tests should fail before the fix.

Then edit.

That gave me something to evaluate before a large AI-generated diff appeared.

ChatGPT became useful in a slightly different role.

I used it for second opinions, architecture review, repository audits, debugging ideas, and eventually as an outside reviewer.

That separation turned out to matter.

When one AI has been working inside a codebase for a while, it starts inheriting the assumptions already present in the project.

A fresh model sometimes asks the annoying question nobody inside the project is asking anymore.

One of those questions uncovered the worst bug I had.

More on that shortly.

I want to be very clear about what “I built this with AI” means in practice.

AI did a lot of thisI still had to own this

Refactor modulesAccept or reject the design

I am comfortable calling the project vibe-coded.

I think pretending otherwise would make the story less interesting.

The important question is what happens after the code appears.

My first version worked surprisingly quickly

Once the basic architecture was settled, AI made the first build fast.

The project grew into separate modules for the conversation flow, scheme loading, rule evaluation, Telegram transport, rendering, application sheets, metrics, and later WhatsApp.

I also ended up with an unusual constraint:

zero third-party Python dependencies.

This was not originally some philosophical goal.

Python's standard library already gave me almost everything I needed:

urllib for HTTP, sqlite3 for storage, tomllib for scheme files, http.server for simple serving, dataclasses for profiles, plus the normal cryptographic and utility modules.

So I kept going that way.

It made the code longer in some places.

It also made deployment remarkably simple.

There was no production dependency resolver to debug.

No Python package versions drifting between my laptop and AWS.

A fresh Python installation could run the application.

At this point I had something that looked good in the repository.

Then I actually used it.

That changed everything.

The first hour of real use found bugs my tests had missed

This was probably the most important debugging session in the project.

I opened the Telegram bot and tried to behave like a normal user instead of like the person who built it.

Things broke almost immediately.

One screen silently died because the Telegram payload included a keyboard field with a null value that Telegram rejected.

The free-text occupation path could trap me in a loop.

The occupation matcher understood nice English and Hindi categories but not the kind of Hinglish people actually type, such as “mistri” or “driver ka kaam.”

There was no proper zero-income option.

One English screen displayed English text with Hindi buttons underneath it.

The generated application sheet still contained hard-coded Hindi in places.

None of these were deep computer-science failures.

That was the point.

I had spent most of my attention on the deterministic rule engine because that was the “important” part.

The rule engine was fine.

The interface around it was not.

The tests were checking the parts that were easiest to assert.

The user was touching different parts.

That session eventually led to a much more aggressive conversation test that walks through reachable buttons and screens rather than checking only a few happy paths.

It also changed the way I think about automated testing.

A green test suite can mean:

everything we tested works.

It cannot tell you:

we tested everything that matters.

One Telegram double-tap could change somebody's eligibility

One bug was much more serious.

Telegram inline keyboards remain active after they are sent unless you explicitly retire them.

My callback payloads originally contained only the answer.

Something like:

yes
no
unknown

The callback did not identify which question the button belonged to.

Imagine this sequence.

The bot asks:

Do you have a bank account?

The worker taps Yes.

The network is slow.

Nothing visibly changes for a moment.

So the worker taps Yes again.

But between those two taps, the conversation state has advanced.

Now the next question might be:

Do you pay income tax?

The second tap arrives as another bare yes.

The application reads the current state.

Suddenly the user has answered yes to a question they never intended to answer.

Some pension schemes exclude income-tax payers.

A harmless-looking double-tap could therefore change an eligibility result.

That is the sort of bug that changed my view of the intended user.

On my laptop with a stable connection, double-tapping a Telegram button looks unnecessary.

On an inexpensive phone with a slow network, pressing again because nothing happened is perfectly normal.

The fix was not “tell users not to double-tap.”

The adapter had to know which message contained the currently active keyboard and reject callbacks from stale keyboards.

Old keyboards were retired before processing the answer.

The system had to assume that normal user behaviour would include impatience, delays, retries and accidental taps.

That is not an edge case.

That is the environment.

My age parser turned 9.5 into 95

Another bug looked almost funny until I thought about what the number controlled.

The early age parser pulled digits out of whatever the user entered.

Conceptually it did this:

digits = "".join(ch for ch in answer if ch.isdigit())

That meant:

9.5 → 95
-5  → 5

So the software was “helpfully” repairing invalid input instead of rejecting it.

The worker might type one thing and get screened using another.

The correct fix was much simpler:

do not repair the input.

Reject it.

At the same time, I did not want to make the parser ASCII-only. Hindi users may type Devanagari digits.

So the solution needed to reject punctuation and malformed values while still accepting valid decimal digits from the scripts the audience may actually use.

That bug gave me a principle I reused elsewhere:

Never silently transform a consequential user answer into something they did not say.

Then ChatGPT found the bug that embarrassed me most

At one point I asked ChatGPT to review the repository from the outside.

A lot of AI code review output is noise.

It will repeat comments already in your code.

It will suggest architecture changes that ignore constraints.

It will sometimes confidently explain a trade-off you deliberately made as though it is a new discovery.

But one finding was devastatingly correct.

I had a property that effectively meant:

@property
def is_verified(self):
    return not self.stubs

In other words:

if the scheme contained no remaining "TODO" values, it was considered verified.

Except that is not what “verified” means.

It meant somebody had finished researching the file.

Nobody had necessarily checked that research carefully enough to put a real worker through it.

The scheme files themselves still said they were pending human verification.

My documentation warned that they should not yet be used for real decisions.

The runtime did not care.

The runtime saw “no TODOs” and returned real verdicts.

I had written the safety rule in prose and failed to put it in the one place that mattered: the execution path.

The fix separated three different states:

researched
human verified
servable

A scheme becomes servable only when it is researched and carries a real human sign-off.

Until then the engine returns:

UNKNOWN

Even if every threshold looks complete.

When I shipped that correction, the demo temporarily became worse.

More answers became UNKNOWN.

That was exactly the correct result.

I would rather have an unimpressive demo that refuses to answer than a polished demo that pretends research and verification are the same thing.

This is also the moment where ChatGPT genuinely changed the quality of the project.

I had written the bug.

Claude-assisted tests had lived around it.

I had read the code repeatedly.

A fresh reviewer noticed the assumption.

That is why I now like using different models as different reviewers instead of expecting one agent to generate, review and approve its own work indefinitely.

Government schemes did not fit my neat data model

I initially designed the scheme format and then researched the real schemes.

That order was educational because reality immediately attacked my assumptions.

For example, I had assumed every scheme could be represented as some kind of financial benefit.

Then I looked closely at e-Shram.

e-Shram is mainly a registration and gateway.

Giving it a fake cash value just so it fits the model would be misleading.

So the data model gained a gateway type.

Then I ran into insurance.

A ₹2 lakh accident cover is not equivalent to ₹2 lakh of annual income.

If I add an insurance cover to a pension and show one “total benefit” number, the result looks impressive and is economically meaningless.

So payouts and insurance cover became separate categories.

Later, one-time support created another category.

The data model was being shaped by the government sources rather than forcing government programmes into whatever abstraction I had invented on day one.

That process repeated with eligibility too.

PM-SYM and e-Shram do not treat every social-security membership identically.

An earlier version tried to compress multiple memberships into one convenient Boolean.

It was neat.

It was also wrong.

So the profile split them.

Every new field meant another possible question for the worker, which made me think much more carefully about whether a distinction belonged in the model.

The goal was never maximum abstraction.

The goal was the smallest model capable of representing the rules honestly.

I stopped letting AI fill government facts

This became another hard boundary.

AI could write the code that uses a scheme threshold.

It could help format a TOML file.

It could suggest where a missing condition might exist.

But I did not want a model inventing the threshold itself.

Government scheme facts are exactly the kind of information where a plausible wrong answer is dangerous.

So I started reading official pages, FAQs, PIB releases, ministry documents and scheme documents myself.

If I could not establish a value, I left it unresolved.

If two sources disagreed, that disagreement went into the documentation.

If a source disappeared, the scheme could lose its signature again.

That actually happened.

A signed scheme is not permanently trusted just because it was trusted yesterday.

On September 15, I corrected several pension values based on better evidence. Because the underlying facts changed, the affected signatures were withdrawn.

I then re-audited and re-signed the ones I could support.

One Uttarakhand widow-pension file remained unsigned because I still wanted a stronger primary source for its ₹1,500 rate.

That may sound like administrative overhead for a small bot.

But the source trail is part of the product.

If the entire pitch is “this does not guess,” then the project has to be willing to become less complete when the evidence becomes weaker.

The scheme files became reviewable objects

Eventually each scheme file became more than configuration.

It became a small audit record.

A rule includes the field to check, the comparison, the human-readable explanation and the source supporting that value.

A simplified rule might look like:

[[criteria]]
field = "age"
op = "between"
value = [18, 40]

source_url = "..."

This gave me a useful property.

Somebody reviewing the welfare rule does not need to understand the Python engine.

They can inspect the scheme file and the source beside it.

I later added a review tool that walks through a scheme and displays the values and their sources before allowing a human signature.

The tool does not “verify” anything automatically.

That would recreate the same trust problem under a nicer interface.

It simply makes human verification harder to skip.

I also started keeping copies or fingerprints of important official source material because government URLs are not as permanent as software developers sometimes imagine.

During the project I encountered pages that changed, pages that returned errors, legacy TLS problems, huge scanned PDFs and citations that disappeared.

The repository therefore became partly an application and partly a record of how its claims were established.

My impact number was wrong because I mixed different kinds of money

This bug was not in a user-facing eligibility result.

That made it more dangerous.

I had an impact metric intended to describe the value of schemes surfaced by the bot.

At one point it effectively added numbers like:

₹36,000 yearly pension
+
₹2,00,000 accident insurance cover

and produced one total.

Mathematically, addition works.

Semantically, it is nonsense.

The ₹36,000 pension is an annual payout.

The ₹2 lakh accident insurance is contingent cover. A person does not receive ₹2 lakh every year.

Combining them massively inflated what the project appeared to be surfacing.

A crash would have been easier to spot.

This produced a beautiful, believable number.

That is much worse.

So the system now treats financial categories separately.

Annual payout stays annual payout.

Insurance cover stays insurance cover.

In-kind or gateway schemes are described without inventing financial value.

One-time support does not quietly become annual income.

I also stopped using phrases like “money delivered” when the system had merely shown somebody an entitlement.

Eligibility is not approval.

Approval is not payment.

A generated application sheet is not a submitted application.

Those distinctions now sound obvious to me.

They became obvious because I got them wrong first.

I managed to fix the pension total and still double-count it somewhere else

Later I ran into a more subtle version of the same problem.

Some pension routes are alternatives.

If one person satisfies two related pension records that represent the same underlying payment, the worker-facing calculation must not simply add both.

I fixed that in the eligibility/value calculation using an exclusive grouping mechanism.

The worker saw the correct total.

Then I discovered the dashboard had its own calculation.

It did not know about the grouping logic.

So the worker-facing system could be correct while my impact statistics were inflated.

That failure bothered me more than if both had been wrong.

If a user sees an obviously wrong result, they can complain.

If an internal dashboard overstates your impact, it can quietly travel into a presentation, a hackathon submission or a portfolio claim.

The lesson was simple:

Do not implement the same business meaning twice just because the second copy is “only reporting.”

Reporting code can lie just as effectively as product code.

I tried to build privacy into the data model

Government benefits can reveal a lot about somebody even if you never ask their name.

Income band.

Widowhood.

Disability.

Pension status.

Employment category.

These are personal facts.

So I tried to collect as little identifying information as possible.

There is no name field in the profile.

No Aadhaar field.

No phone-number field.

No exact address.

This is different from saying:

We promise not to store your Aadhaar.

There is nowhere in the profile to put it.

The transient conversation does need a channel identifier to route messages, because Telegram and WhatsApp cannot function otherwise.

But the metrics database is deliberately separate.

It stores coarse categories and random session identifiers rather than a person's messaging identity.

I liked this design.

Then I accidentally violated the privacy model outside the Python application.

The privacy bug was in Caddy, not Python

The application can generate a temporary result/application sheet.

To make the sheet easy to open from a phone, I added a short-lived URL with a random token.

The Python server intentionally did not log those URLs.

The token was effectively a temporary key to something sensitive.

So far, so good.

Then I put Caddy in front of the Python service for HTTPS.

Caddy saw every request before Python did.

And the default access logging could record both the client's IP address and the tokenized path.

I had carefully protected the information at one layer and leaked the relationship at another layer I had added myself.

Luckily this was found during my own testing rather than after a real worker pilot.

The fix had to happen at the proxy layer by redacting the sensitive path and removing information I did not need.

That taught me something I should have understood earlier:

privacy is a property of the whole system, not a function inside the application.

Your database can be perfectly minimal while your reverse proxy tells a different story.

Deploying to AWS taught me a different kind of debugging

Until this point, much of the work was code-centric.

Then I had to make the bot stay alive.

I deployed Yojana Sathi to a small AWS EC2 instance in ap-south-1.

This part was much more manual than the phrase “AI built my project” suggests.

I had to deal with the instance, SSH access, filesystem locations, service users, environment files, systemd, Caddy, TLS, deployment copies, backups and actual process status.

The production code lives separately from the secrets and SQLite database.

The Telegram bot runs as a service.

WhatsApp has its own service.

Caddy sits in front of the webhook and sheet endpoints.

Every deployment is supposed to run the test suite on the target machine before the live service is restarted.

That last detail matters because my laptop and the server do not necessarily run the exact same Python environment.

Running tests locally tells me my laptop likes the code.

It does not prove the server does.

Deployment found bugs that reading the code had not.

For example, the systemd service initially buffered Python output, so the logs looked empty even when the bot was running.

Some systemd rate-limit options were placed in the wrong section and were silently ignored.

An update script started a stopped service correctly but failed to restart an already-running service, meaning I could deploy new files and continue running old code.

Nothing about those bugs was glamorous.

All of them matter more to a live service than another architecture diagram.

I also learned not to test a live Telegram poller by poking it randomly

At one point I wanted to confirm whether the production Telegram process was really polling.

I reasoned that Telegram allows one long poller, so making my own getUpdates request should produce a conflict if the service was already running.

The request succeeded.

I concluded the service was down.

My conclusion was wrong.

The diagnostic itself interfered with the behaviour I was trying to observe.

I had created a testing method based on an assumption about Telegram's polling semantics that was not true.

Eventually I relied on evidence such as the actual process connection and fresh event activity instead of interpreting one API response too confidently.

There is a nice symmetry there.

I had built a product whose rule was:

do not guess when information is incomplete.

Then I guessed about my own infrastructure.

Adding WhatsApp was less about code than integration boundaries

Telegram was the obvious first channel because it is easy to build and test.

WhatsApp mattered more strategically because it is where many of the people I am designing for already communicate.

The good news was that the channel separation worked.

The WhatsApp adapter could reuse the same conversation and the exact same rule engine.

A worker's eligibility should not change because a message arrived through Telegram rather than WhatsApp.

But the platform constraints were different.

WhatsApp has different button/list limits, webhook requirements, signatures, message formats and account setup.

I had to configure the Meta side, work with the Cloud API, set up the webhook and test the conversation on a real phone using Meta's test setup.

The WhatsApp path now works end to end against the test number.

It is deliberately not described as a public production WhatsApp service yet.

That would require the remaining Meta/business setup and a real public number.

This distinction is another one I care about.

“Code exists” is not the same as “feature is publicly available.”

Telegram is live.

WhatsApp is built and tested but not yet publicly launched.

Both statements can be true at the same time.

I eventually changed the conversation itself

The early bot asked a broad intake and then checked every available scheme.

That worked, but it created a usability problem.

Suppose someone only wants to check PMSBY.

Why should they answer questions needed exclusively for a widow pension, an LPG scheme or a trader pension?

The rule engine already knew which profile fields each scheme uses because the criteria are stored in the scheme files.

So I changed the conversation.

After consent, the worker can now check all verified schemes or select the schemes they actually care about.

The conversation planner looks at those selected schemes and derives the smallest relevant set of questions.

This was important because I did not want the conversation layer to copy the rules.

The rule engine remains the source of truth.

The planner only asks:

Which facts might these selected rules need?

It never decides:

What is the correct answer?

Dependencies then remove questions that no longer matter.

If somebody says they are not a woman, a widow-status question can be skipped.

If a household already has an LPG/PNG connection, later Ujjwala-specific questions may no longer be useful.

But an answer of Don't know does not silently become a skip.

Unknown stays unknown.

That feature made the bot feel much more like a product and less like a form.

The testing strategy grew because my green tests kept lying to me

I now have a complicated relationship with the phrase “all tests passed.”

It is useful.

It is not proof.

At different points I had tests that were technically passing while failing to exercise the behaviour their names claimed to protect.

One test compared the wrong message.

Another path walker stopped reaching later screens after a behaviour change but still passed assertions about everything it did reach.

That led to an unusual rule in parts of the suite:

tests should sometimes verify that they actually tested something.

A path test can count whether it reached the end.

A generated-pack test can assert that at least one pack was generated.

A screen walker can verify that the expected states were actually visited.

There are now two broad styles of testing in the project.

One tries to break the user journey by walking conversation paths through Telegram and WhatsApp behaviour.

The other attacks the rule engine by comparing edge cases and boundaries against independently encoded expectations.

They catch different failures.

Pressing every button cannot tell me that an official age threshold is wrong.

Checking every age boundary cannot tell me the English button is accidentally written in Hindi.

I need both.

And even then, I still need to use the bot myself.

Documentation became part of how I worked with AI

One unexpected side effect of using Claude Code heavily was that documentation stopped being something I planned to write after finishing the project.

It became working memory.

AI sessions lose context.

Humans lose context too.

After several days, I could no longer rely on remembering why a strange condition existed.

So the repository accumulated documents.

Architecture notes.

A build log.

Source reviews.

Verification worksheets.

Pilot planning.

Impact methodology.

Bug histories.

Deployment runbooks.

Handoff notes.

Some of those documents contradict older ones because the project changed.

I decided not to clean every contradiction out of history.

Instead I started marking old documents as snapshots and keeping newer progress files as the current state.

That makes the repo messier.

It also makes it much more useful to me.

I can see what I believed on September 7 and why it changed on September 10.

I can see which source broke.

I can see why a field was added.

I can see a bug fix after the bug rather than only the final code.

That became especially useful when working with AI.

Instead of starting a new session with:

Understand my entire project.

I could point the model at the architecture, the current progress log and the relevant source review.

The documentation became context infrastructure.

Where Yojana Sathi stands now

As I write this, Yojana Sathi is live on Telegram.

The production system has 10 signed schemes currently serving real deterministic verdicts.

Other researched or drafted schemes deliberately remain unavailable to workers until their review is complete.

The Uttarakhand widow pension is one example of why that distinction exists: I withdrew its signature while I look for stronger primary evidence for the current benefit amount.

That means the project can move backwards from “verified” to “unknown.”

I consider that a feature.

The bot is deployed on AWS.

Selected-scheme screening is live.

The rule engine, Telegram path and WhatsApp path share the same underlying conversation logic.

The WhatsApp integration has been tested end to end using Meta's test environment, but it is not yet a public WhatsApp number.

There is still no real field-pilot evidence.

That last sentence matters more than most of the engineering work above it.

The bot has been tested extensively by me.

That is not the same as testing it with the people it was built for.

A real worker may interpret a Hindi sentence differently.

A question I think is obvious may be confusing.

The application sheet may make sense to me and not to somebody standing at a CSC.

The Telegram requirement itself may be too much friction.

Those are no longer programming questions.

And I do not want to replace them with synthetic evidence just because synthetic evidence is easier to generate.

What “vibe coding” taught me

Before this project, it was easy for me to think of AI coding as mainly a question of productivity.

How much faster can I make software?

How many files can Claude change in one prompt?

How quickly can I get from an idea to a working demo?

Yojana Sathi changed the question.

Generating code became cheap.

Trusting the result did not.

In fact, faster code generation sometimes made verification more important.

When writing code slowly myself, I naturally spend time thinking while typing.

When an AI changes six files in seconds, I can suddenly own a lot of behaviour I have never personally reasoned through.

That does not mean AI-generated code is bad.

Some of the best parts of this repository were written with AI.

It means the bottleneck moves.

The job becomes defining constraints, reading diffs, designing tests, reproducing failures, verifying real-world facts, operating the system and deciding which output you are willing to stand behind.

That is why I am comfortable saying:

I vibe-coded Yojana Sathi.

And I am equally comfortable saying:

I built Yojana Sathi.

Those statements are not contradictory to me.

I did not build it by manually typing every character.

I built it by making the decisions the software now represents, repeatedly breaking what the AI produced, keeping the fixes I could defend, rejecting things I could not verify, and eventually putting the system on an actual server.

The authorship of keystrokes is much less interesting than the ownership of decisions.

The part I am most proud of is that the bot can refuse me

A funny thing happened during this project.

At the beginning, I wanted to add more schemes.

More features.

More channels.

More polish.

By the end, the behaviour I valued most was the one that looked least impressive:

UNKNOWN

I do not know.

This source is not strong enough.

You did not provide enough information.

These two official sources disagree.

A human has not signed this scheme yet.

Please ask this question at the office.

That is not normally how software demos are designed.

Demos want confident answers.

Hackathons want big numbers.

Portfolios want finished projects.

But the most important property I managed to build into Yojana Sathi is that an unfinished answer is allowed to remain unfinished.

I had to learn that rule more than once myself.

A passing test did not necessarily mean the test was useful.

A running process did not necessarily mean the deployment was healthy.

A complete scheme file did not mean the scheme was verified.

A large rupee number did not necessarily mean real value delivered.

A working WhatsApp adapter did not mean WhatsApp was publicly launched.

My own project kept forcing me to distinguish what looked finished from what I could actually prove.

That is probably the real story of Yojana Sathi.

Not that I built a welfare chatbot with AI.

That part was surprisingly easy.

The harder part was deciding when the software had earned the right to answer.

What comes next

I do not think the next important milestone is another feature.

It is putting the current system in front of people who did not build it.

The Hindi needs independent human review.

The conversation needs to be tested on the kinds of phones and network conditions it was designed for.

Workers need to tell me which questions make sense and which do not.

And the project needs to measure outcomes without quietly upgrading “screened” into “helped,” or “eligible” into “received a benefit.”

If that testing finds that the whole approach is wrong, I want to know.

That would be more useful than another thousand lines of code.

There are still more schemes I could add.

The architecture can support them.

AI could generate the draft files and supporting code very quickly.

But one of the rules I learned while building this is:

coverage grows when a scheme can be trusted, not when another TOML file appears in Git.

So I am trying to resist the easiest form of progress.

Final thought

I started this project thinking AI would be the interesting part.

It wrote almost all of the code.

It saved me an enormous amount of time.

Claude Code let me work across a repository at a scale I would not have managed this quickly alone.

ChatGPT gave me a second perspective and found a verification failure I had missed.

I would use both again.

But if I had to explain what I learned from Yojana Sathi in one sentence, it would not be about AI.

It would be this:

Before you build a system that gives people an answer, decide what happens when the system does not deserve to be confident.

For Yojana Sathi, that answer became UNKNOWN.

And strangely enough, teaching a vibe-coded bot to say “I don't know” ended up teaching me the same thing.
